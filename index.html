Mission briefing

Operation Last Horizon

A small experimental station sits near the Moon's south pole. It studies sunlight at the pole, tests the use of lunar ice and asks one question: can people live here?

Your crew has just landed. The base is only partly working: not enough power for the whole lunar day, the antenna needs repair and reserves are limited. You command the operations. LUMA, the station's support system, reads the data with you.

    The story is fiction. Sunlight, terrain and space weather are real NASA measurements (9 Oct 2024 scenario).

    How to play

    • Time runs on its own: 2 game hours per second. Survive 708 hours with at least one of your 3 lives.

    • Send the rover on missions: lunar ice becomes water, new solar arrays bring power, the antenna warns you about storms.

    • Sunlight at the pole is real (JPL Horizons). When the Sun drops behind the measured horizon, the battery must last the night.

    • Space weather is real (NASA DONKI). During radiation, keep the crew inside.

    • If power, air, water or food stays at zero for 6 hours, you lose a life.

    • Drag to orbit the camera, scroll to zoom. Esc pauses. The toolbar at the bottom opens each system.

    Real data inside the game

    Explore the science

    Sunlight at the pole

    At the south pole the Sun circles the horizon, very low. The yellow path is its real position for 709 hours (NASA/JPL Horizons). The blue line is the horizon measured by the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter laser altimeter (LRO LOLA). Where the path is above the line, your panels get light.

    Why it matters to you

    The base is lit for the first 370 hours, then the Sun hides behind the terrain for almost 280 hours. Install panels early and store energy for that long night.

    Water from lunar ice

    Ice in permanently shadowed craters is mixed with regolith. To get water you dig the icy soil, heat it so the ice turns to vapour, capture and condense the vapour, then purify it. The game shows this as the rover's ice mission.

    Space weather

    Solar storms and particle events come from NASA DONKI records of 9 Oct 2024. Storms cut panel output; radiation stops outdoor work.

    Illustrative: rover, greenhouse, consumption rates, ice site position and terrain height exaggeration (2×).
